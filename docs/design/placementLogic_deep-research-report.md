# Critical Teardown of a Bayesian Alignment Engine for Magic Factions

## 2026-06-13 current-state update

This report still holds as an architectural critique, but its numeric premise is now stale. The live placement model is no longer a 32-label or 36-label problem. Current `data/placement-model.json` reports **37 active expressions**:

- 5 mono colors
- 10 Ravnica guild expressions
- 5 Strixhaven college expressions
- 5 shards
- 5 wedges
- 5 four-color expressions
- `WUBRG`
- `COLORLESS`

The current live direct-faction placement model is internally green: `npm.cmd run test:placement` passes with **37 factions and 37 golden paths**. That means the existing engine should be treated as the working baseline while the proposed vector Gate remains research-only.

The 37-expression Gate research rerun in `gate-phase-compression-framework.md` updates the implementation conclusion:

- The WUBRG-vector Gate still fits as a Gate replacement direction.
- `sqrt` propagation with Gate `bmp = 0` remains the best color-bearing setting.
- The Appendix A vector Gate reaches **36/37** under color-only propagation; the miss is `COLORLESS`.
- `COLORLESS` cannot be represented by WUBRG vectors alone and needs explicit outside-WUBRG boundary evidence or a documented calibration exception.
- `WUBRG` should not be scored as a normal additive five-color leaf; it needs balanced all-five integration.
- Same-color guild/college duplicate pairs remain tied under a color-only Gate, which confirms that Hall and Crucible must stay faction-specific.

So the report's core warning is stronger, not weaker: do not abandon Bayesian classification, but do not make the Gate a flat direct-faction lookup or a naive additive color accumulator. The right implementation target is a WUBRG-vector Gate plus special boundary/integration channels, followed by faction-specific Hall and Crucible resolution.

## Bottom line

As specified, this is not yet the best possible architecture for the problem you described. The strongest idea in your design is the adaptive evidence race itself: Bayesian updating is a natural fit for interpretive classification, and adaptive testing literature strongly supports selecting later items based on earlier uncertainty rather than treating all respondents as if they need the same questions. But the current proposal has three structural weaknesses that will hurt accuracy before you even get to item writing: your taxonomy count is internally inconsistent, your flat equal-prior race does not adequately control for model flexibility, and a fixed four-question Gate is probably too short to reliably form a stable shortlist when the labels are this overlapping. Adaptive testing gains its efficiency from carefully chosen items, content constraints, and principled stopping rules, not from shortness by itself. citeturn16view8turn20view2turn18view6turn29view2turn29view3

There is also a hidden configuration problem: the label inventory you wrote out is not a 32-label set. If you include all the categories you listed, it is **36 labels**: 5 monocolor + 10 guilds + 5 Strixhaven colleges + 5 shards + 5 wedges + 5 four-color + 1 five-color. That matters because the math changes, and because the task is not a single flat “color combination” problem. It is really **31 unique mana combinations** plus **5 duplicated two-color expressions** where the same mana pair must split into different institutions and philosophies. In other words, you are solving a layered classification problem, not just a 32-way or 36-way flat race. That layered nature is exactly what official Magic design history emphasizes: factions succeed when they have a unique voice, outlook, and feel, and they fail when those distinctions blur. citeturn34view1turn34view2

My verdict is that a **hybrid hierarchical-Bayesian architecture** is better than the current flat race: keep Bayesian updating, but move to a layered ontology, let four-color and five-color nodes behave like composite hypotheses with built-in complexity penalties, and drive Hall and Crucible with expected information gain or pairwise Bayes factors rather than “softmax closeness” alone. That gives you the interpretability and uncertainty handling of Bayes, the overlap handling of vector embeddings, and the faction distinctness your lore mapping actually needs. Bayesian model comparison is particularly useful here because it naturally applies an Occam penalty to more flexible models instead of letting broad composite explanations win just because they can fit more kinds of people. citeturn32view0turn38search0turn16view3turn25view0

## Mathematical and configurational risk

The biggest mathematical danger is exactly the one you identified: four-color and five-color hypotheses can become **statistical sinks**. Official Magic design commentary says four-color designs tend to feel “soup-y” and hard to define because there is no single color focus, and it also says the identity of a four-color card is fundamentally tied to the **missing** color, which is difficult to express cleanly in normal positive color-pie design. If your likelihood model treats large-color factions as additive supersets of their constituents, they will inherit an unfair coverage advantage. A respondent who is strongly Blue/Black/Red can look like Grixis, Glint-Eye, or Five-Color unless the model has an explicit way to interpret *missing White* and *missing Green* as distinct evidence-bearing facts rather than mere absences. citeturn16view3turn25view0

This is why equal priors across all leaf labels are not enough. Equal priors only make the starting line look fair; they do not make the race fair if some hypotheses are more flexible than others. In Bayesian model comparison, more flexible models are supposed to be checked by their **marginal likelihood**, which rewards fit but penalizes unused flexibility. MacKay’s exposition of Bayesian model comparison is useful here: Bayesian evidence automatically embodies Occam’s razor, favoring simpler models when a more complex one spreads its predictions too widely. Your current flat race needs something functionally equivalent, or your large composite factions will keep overperforming for the wrong reason. citeturn33view0turn38search0

The cleanest fix is to stop treating four-color and five-color labels as ordinary point prototypes. They should be **composite models**. In practice, that means monocolor, guild, college, shard, and wedge labels can be scored as direct faction prototypes, while four-color and five-color labels are evaluated by integrating over latent internal color weights and by requiring affirmative evidence about the excluded color. Conceptually:

\[
p(\mathbf{y}\mid h_{4c})=\int p(\mathbf{y}\mid \mathbf{w}, h_{4c}, a_{\text{missing}})\,p(\mathbf{w},a_{\text{missing}}\mid h_{4c})\,d\mathbf{w}\,da
\]

where \(\mathbf{w}\) are the weights of the included colors and \(a_{\text{missing}}\) is a positive “anti-missing-color” factor. This way, “non-White” is not just “contains UBRG”; it is “contains UBRG **and** shows patterned resistance to White’s moral-regulatory package.” That is much closer to how Magic itself talks about four-color identity. citeturn25view0turn33view0

The Gate is your second bottleneck. If there are truly 32 active labels, the prior entropy is \( \log_2(32)=5 \) bits. If you keep every label you listed, it is \( \log_2(36)\approx 5.17 \) bits. If the Gate only needs to reduce the field to a stable top three, then in the noiseless best case you still want posterior entropy roughly at or below \( \log_2(3)\approx 1.585 \) bits. That means the Gate must remove about **3.4 bits** of uncertainty in the 32-label version, or about **3.6 bits** in the 36-label version. Dividing by four fixed questions, you are asking each Gate item to contribute roughly **0.85 to 0.90 effective bits** on average. That is a very high requirement for broad human-facing questions about philosophy, identity, and lore-safe self-description. Shannon gives you the ceiling; psychometric reality, content balancing, and overlap drag the realized information downward. citeturn16view5turn20view2turn20view0turn29view2

So can four Gate questions produce a meaningful top three? In principle, yes. In practice, only if those questions are engineered as **high-information contrast items** that are near-orthogonal, category-rich, and carefully calibrated. Otherwise, no. Short adaptive diagnostic testing research exists precisely because fixed short tests often need mutual-information or KL-based item selection to classify reliably under tight length limits. The moment your items are broad enough to feel natural, they also become more correlated and less discriminative. That is why I would not keep Gate fixed at four no matter what. I would make it **minimum four, maximum six**, with an entropy stop rule rather than a hard count. citeturn29view2turn29view3turn18view6turn20view2

There is a third math risk that will quietly distort the posteriors: **double counting through local dependence**. If your Gate and Hall items are semantically adjacent—say, three differently worded prompts all measuring “institutional trust” or “self-authored identity”—a naive product-of-likelihoods update will overstate the evidence. Item response theory and adaptive testing literature are explicit that local independence is a cornerstone assumption; once that assumption is violated, multiplying item likelihoods can exaggerate confidence. That is especially dangerous in a lore-forward test where multiple items can end up paraphrasing the same color philosophy in different skins. citeturn20view0turn29view0

The practical conclusion is blunt: your present flat race is mathematically elegant on paper, but it needs three upgrades before it is trustworthy. It needs **taxonomy normalization**, **complexity-aware composite modeling**, and a **variable-length Gate with dependence-aware item design**. Without those, your leaderboard will often look more decisive than it really is. citeturn20view2turn29view0turn32view0

## Taxonomical rigor and lore mapping

The lore problem is not a side issue; it is the heart of the classifier. Wizards’ own design writing on factions says successful factions need a unique voice, outlook, and style of play, and that blur between factions damages the sense of uniqueness. Rosewater also explicitly said that Ravnica’s guilds were the “lowest hanging fruit” of the two-color pairs: the obvious expressions of those pairs, made even more legible because Ravnica was built to play up their identities. Strixhaven, by contrast, was designed specifically to prove that a two-color faction set could have a **very different identity from Ravnica** while using the same color pairs. That means your engine has to model “color pair” and “institutional expression of that color pair” as separate but linked layers. citeturn34view1turn34view2turn20view3turn16view0

That distinction comes into focus with **Izzet versus Prismari**. Official Ravnica material describes Izzet as invention-and-discovery-first, impatient, reckless, and civically consequential, with experimental chaos tied to technology and engineering. Official Strixhaven material describes Prismari as artistic training, artistic theory, emotional truth, performance, and elemental magic as living art; its Blue side is technique and theory, and its Red side is expressive display and emotional truth. Those are not small flavor differences. They are different *teloi*. Izzet asks, “What can experimentation discover or build?” Prismari asks, “What can expression reveal or make people feel?” citeturn37view4turn16view1turn37view2turn37view3

So your “biological expression placement” cannot stop at “Blue-Red equals X.” To separate those labels cleanly, the mapping has to change on at least three axes. First, the **moral frame** should diverge: Izzet is about epistemic novelty and applied discovery, while Prismari is about aesthetic truth and expressive realization. Second, the **risk signal** should diverge: Izzet risk is tolerated as an externality of experimentation, while Prismari risk is tolerated as the emotional and performative cost of sublime expression. Third, the **inhibitor trigger** should diverge: Izzet chafes at prolonged process drag and anti-innovation bureaucracy, while Prismari chafes at sterile formalism, derivative work, or technique without soul. If you do not encode those differences, the labels will collapse into the same evidence basin. citeturn37view4turn16view1turn37view2turn37view3

The same problem appears in your example, **Azorius = The Institutional Stabilizer**. Official Azorius lore is overtly about legislation, enforcement, arbitration, and the idea that law is the barrier against chaos, even at the cost of bureaucracy and occasional injustice. That is a different social form from generic White-Blue idealism. A classifier that answers only “How White?” and “How Blue?” will miss the institutional emphasis. The lore-congruent variable is not just *order* or *optimization*; it is *formalized order administered through systems*. citeturn16view2turn21view1turn22view5

This is also where I think your current psych framing is too thin. One moral foundation, one risk signal, and one inhibitor trigger will not reliably separate 36 labels, especially when five of those labels duplicate existing color pairs. Psychometric scale design literature emphasizes clear conceptualization and careful item wording, and recent work on the Moral Foundations Questionnaire shows that the classic five-foundation structure has substantial measurement problems and fits better under revised or bifactor structures. In other words, moral foundations can be one informative layer, but they are not a stable enough master key to carry the whole taxonomy. If you center too much on them, your classifier will inherit their measurement ambiguity. citeturn18view3turn35view0turn18view0

Your “risk signal” and “inhibitor trigger” ideas are stronger, but only if they are grounded in something like revised Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory rather than purely bespoke lore language. Revised RST specifically distinguishes approach, inhibition, and fight/flight/freeze tendencies, and there are validated instruments built around those constructs. That makes RST-like features a much better psychometric substrate for your “risk” and “inhibitor” channels than an unvalidated lore intuition alone. I would still keep the Magic-facing names, but under the hood I would anchor them to validated approach/avoidance constructs. citeturn36search0turn36search6

The **four-color problem** is where positive evidence modeling becomes most delicate. Wizards’ own design commentary says the identity of a four-color group is fundamentally about the missing color, and also admits that this is hard because color-pie design normally works by presence, not by absence. The way around that is to stop treating “absence” as empty space. Make it a **positive contrast channel**. Non-White is not “all colors except White”; it is “a pattern that declines White’s package of peace-through-law, moral bright lines, external obligation, and social guilt as a regulatory tool.” Official White philosophy material emphasizes peace, clear rules, consequences, and the moral instruction of the community; the absence of White can therefore be probed with active preferences for ambiguity, self-authored norms, conflict tolerance, or distaste for procedural restraint. citeturn25view0turn22view5turn16view2turn20view6

That same logic lets you distinguish **Grixis**, **Glint-Eye non-White**, and **Five-Color**. A strong UBR respondent who also rejects White’s regulatory package but does **not** show meaningful Green signals about purpose, instinct, natural role, or acceptance should drift toward Grixis. If that same respondent also shows affirmative Green signals, then Glint-Eye becomes viable. Five-Color should only win if the respondent shows at least moderate positive compatibility with *all five* foundational vocabularies, including White’s peace/order logic and Green’s acceptance/purpose logic. If Five-Color can win without White and Green being positively evidenced, it is not a true five-color hypothesis; it is just an overbroad sink. citeturn22view3turn20view8turn22view5turn21view1turn21view2turn22view2

## User psychology and interview flow

The adaptive structure is not automatically a UX win. Research on computerized adaptive testing shows that CAT is often more efficient than fixed tests, but the psychological effects are mixed. A recent meta-analysis found no overall significant effect of CAT on motivation and anxiety across studies, even though some individual studies did find lower anxiety in adaptive formats. Earlier work also showed that item difficulty patterns and the difficulty of subsequent items can affect perceived performance and test-taker reactions. The lesson for your engine is that “adaptive” is not itself the experience; the *shape of the adaptation* is the experience. citeturn31view2turn19view0turn19view1turn19view2

That matters directly for the Gate–Hall–Crucible transition. If the Gate asks broad, reflective worldview questions and then the Hall suddenly starts asking ultra-specific “gotcha” discriminants, the respondent can feel the test is no longer learning about them but trying to force a result. That is the whiplash you are worried about, and I think it is real. The fix is not to avoid highly discriminative questions; it is to keep the rhetorical register consistent. The Hall should still *feel* like the same test, only more focused. The respondent should be told early that initial questions establish broad patterning and later questions separate close neighbors. Then the later items should sound like refined worldview dilemmas, not like hidden lore traps. citeturn18view4turn19view2turn18view2turn11search3

Face validity is essential here. Instrument-development work repeatedly emphasizes that a measure must appear relevant, clear, and appropriate to its audience, and broader psychometric guidance stresses clear construct definition and careful item wording. If your respondent cannot see why a Hall or Crucible item is about their placement, accuracy will not rescue the experience. In a fandom-adjacent identity test, perceived validity is part of the reward structure. citeturn18view2turn18view3turn11search3

The “never canon” issue is therefore mostly a **results-language** issue, not a scoring issue. If you keep reminding users that the taxonomy is interpretive and unofficial at every turn, the test will feel evasive. But if you pretend it is canonical, lore-savvy users will distrust you. The compromise is simple: present the boundary once, up front, and then be definite *inside the model*. In practice, the result should read like: “Best fit in this model: Prismari. Strong secondary resonance: Izzet. Confidence: moderate.” That preserves payoff while acknowledging uncertainty in a way users can understand. Similar logic is already normal in adaptive testing and model comparison: uncertainty is not a weakness if it is communicated as part of the classifier’s discipline rather than as a hedge. citeturn20view2turn38search0turn18view2

Crucible especially should not be framed as “the one weird tiebreaker.” It should be framed as “the final distinction between two very close expressions.” That preserves dignity. And because close rivalries are where user trust is most fragile, Crucible items should be your most face-valid, most lore-resonant, most pairwise-legible questions in the entire bank. If the user reaches that phase and then gets an item that feels obscure or arbitrary, the whole experience collapses at the exact moment it should feel sharpest. citeturn18view2turn18view3turn19view2

## Alternative architecture comparison

An **elimination tree** has two real advantages. It is easy to explain, and it can be very fast. If you believe the main task is to decide which colors are absent, a tree can get to a rough family quickly. Decision-tree literature has long used information gain for this kind of split selection. For a user-facing product, that clarity is attractive. citeturn18view7

But as a full architecture for your problem, the elimination tree is weaker than it looks. Top-down hierarchical classifiers are prone to **propagation error**: once an early branch is wrong, later evidence often cannot recover the lost path. That may be acceptable in taxonomies where upper-level branches are clean and unambiguous. Your label set is not like that. It contains overlapping composites, same-color duplicate expressions, and labels partly defined by excluded colors. The very things that make Magic alignment interesting are the things that make early hard exclusions dangerous. Even the broader hierarchical-classification literature notes that flat and hierarchical strategies trade off simplicity against propagation error, and that there is no universal reason to prefer a full top-down approach. citeturn32view1turn18view7

A **vector space or cosine similarity model** handles overlap much better. Official vector-space literature treats objects as points in a high-dimensional space and uses the angle between vectors as a similarity metric. For your domain, that is appealing because players really do have mixed profiles, near neighbors, and “secondary resonance.” A vector model is excellent for showing why someone is mostly Jeskai but slightly Prismari, or mostly Azorius with a Quandrix flank. citeturn18view8turn8search0

The problem is that cosine similarity by itself is not a classifier with calibrated uncertainty; it is a similarity score. It can rank candidate factions, but it does not natively tell you when you have enough evidence, how to stop adaptively, or how much a respondent truly separates from their nearest competitor. It also blurs same-color duplicates unless the embedding includes setting-specific and institutional dimensions in addition to color-philosophy dimensions. If you only embed color values, Izzet and Prismari will end up almost on top of each other. citeturn18view8turn16view0turn37view4turn37view3

So the clean verdict is this: **Bayesian race is superior to either alternative only if you upgrade it.** A pure elimination tree is too brittle. A pure vector/cosine model is too fuzzy and not sufficiently probabilistic. The strongest system is a Bayesian classifier *over* a vector or latent-trait representation, with adaptive item selection and layered label structure. That gets you interpretability, overlap handling, honest uncertainty, and efficient question routing. citeturn32view0turn18view6turn29view2turn29view3

## Recommended architecture

The architecture I would endorse is a **hierarchical Bayesian adaptive classifier with a vector-backed latent representation**.

At the ontology level, normalize the label space first. Separate **mana structure** from **expression layer**. That means monocolor, three-color, four-color, and five-color labels live in one branch, while the five duplicated two-color cases split into “Ravnica guild expression” and “Strixhaven college expression” as sibling sublabels rather than pretending they are simply extra unrelated leaves. Official Magic design history supports this move because Strixhaven was intentionally built as a different expression of the same two-color space, not as a different mana combination. citeturn16view0turn20view3turn34view2

At the scoring level, represent ordinary factions as direct prototypes, but model four-color and five-color labels as **composite hypotheses** with latent internal weightings and affirmative excluded-color contrast factors. Use Bayesian evidence or Bayes factors rather than raw softmax gaps to govern close calls, because Bayesian evidence explicitly addresses relative support among competing models and automatically penalizes overflexible explanations. citeturn33view0turn38search0

At the psychometric level, do not let “moral foundation + risk signal + inhibitor trigger” be the whole latent basis. Keep those as interpretive surfaces, but underneath them use a wider construct map that includes at least: regulatory style, temporal horizon, self-authorship versus destined role, institutional affinity, conflict tolerance, epistemic style, and expressive telos. This is the only realistic way to separate same-color alternatives cleanly while preserving lore truth. Scale-construction guidance and faction-design guidance both point in the same direction: clear constructs and strong differentiation are non-negotiable. citeturn18view3turn34view2turn35view0

At the interview-flow level, I would change the stages this way. **Gate** should be variable length, not fixed length: minimum four items, maximum six, stopping when posterior entropy drops below a threshold such as about 1.5 bits or when cumulative mass over the top three crosses a target such as 0.80. **Hall** should choose items by mutual information or KL divergence among the current contenders, while still respecting content balance so you do not ask three semantically redundant questions. **Crucible** should fire only when the top two remain too close under posterior odds or Bayes-factor criteria, not just because a softmax gap looks numerically small. Short-test adaptive diagnosis literature strongly favors mutual-information or KL-based item selection when the assessment budget is tight. citeturn29view2turn29view3turn18view6turn20view2turn38search0

At the UX level, keep the item voice stable from start to finish, foreshadow the narrowing process, and make every late-stage question obviously about a real philosophical distinction. Then present the result in a way that satisfies both certainty and honesty: one primary placement, one or two secondary resonances, and a confidence statement. That is the right tradeoff for an interpretive taxonomy. citeturn18view2turn18view3turn31view2

If you want the shortest version of the recommendation, it is this: **do not abandon Bayesian classification; abandon the idea that a flat, fixed-length, equal-prior leaf race is enough.** Keep Bayes. Add hierarchy, composite evidence, variable stopping, dependence-aware item design, and a richer latent map.

## Open questions and limitations

This critique is structural rather than empirical because I did not have your calibrated item bank, pilot response data, or confusion matrices. Without those, I can identify where the architecture is likely to fail, but I cannot estimate its actual misclassification rate or the true effective information gain of your current Gate items. Adaptive-testing standards are very clear that content coverage, item calibration, and stopping rules all have to be validated empirically, not only argued conceptually. citeturn20view2turn16view8

The other major unresolved issue is the taxonomy itself. As written, your label list is 36 rather than 32, and it mixes unique mana combinations with duplicate same-pair cultural expressions. That mismatch is not a minor bookkeeping error; it changes the entropy math and the architecture choice. Resolve that first, and much of the rest of the design will become cleaner.
