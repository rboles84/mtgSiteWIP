# Executive Research Value

- Overall research value: Very High.
- Whether this source adds genuinely new evidence: Some.
- Whether this source was worth processing: Absolutely.

This source is valuable because players explain what kinds of advice changed their deckbuilding and why. The strongest evidence is not simply that people learned to add lands, draw, removal, or ramp; it is that players distinguish functional improvement from ownership, social fit, bracket fit, and fun. The source is unusually rich for recommendation-success and recommendation-failure evidence because commenters identify which lessons became usable, which felt too generic, and which created anxiety or pod mismatch. It mostly reinforces recurring Commander concerns around consistency, synergy, staples, and power calibration, while adding unusually specific evidence about YouTube as a learning engine, search-literacy gateway, and sometimes a source of prescriptive pressure.

## Tool Literacy, Discovery, and Ownership

### Observation: Scryfall literacy turns deckbuilding into discovery

Evidence type: Recommendation success; Building preference; Desired experience

Representative player language: “your scryfall guide completely opened up the world of deck building to me”

Synthesis: Many players describe Scryfall syntax, o-tags, Scryfall Tagger, and search guides as major unlocks. The improvement is not only finding stronger cards; it is escaping copied lists and discovering obscure cards that feel personally chosen.

Why it matters: Search skill can create agency. Recommendation systems should help players discover and justify cards, not only hand them finished lists.

Support within this source: Strong repeated pattern

Why it matters: “Uses EDHREC” should not be read as passive netdecking. Players use the same tool for copying, filtering, comparison, and independent search.

Variation or counterexample: Some players still prefer EDHREC as the most practical starting point before playtesting.

# Recommendation Successes

- Search literacy: Scryfall, tags, and filtering helped players discover personal cards and stop copying lists.
- Resource fundamentals: land counts, curve-aware ramp, and draw improved playability and reduced non-games.
- Intentionality: players learned to ask what each card does for the plan.
- Win-condition planning: building backward from the ending improved deck coherence.
- Counterweight commanders: commanders used to fill gaps solved specific shell problems.
- Social/gameplay framing: some players learned threat assessment, politics, table mood, and power calibration.
- Structural tools: 30/33-card frameworks, calculators, and package testing helped overwhelmed builders.

# Recommendation Failures

- Advice that is too generic fails specific decks.
- Advice can push a deck into the wrong bracket or local power environment.
- Creator discourse can cause anxiety, confusion, or fear of being judged.
- Optimization can erase the deck’s intended fun if powerful cards replace the core experience.
- EDHREC and “top cards” content can create homogenized lists.
- Some veteran players find most advice obvious and only gain card discovery or perspective.
- Some players are collection-bound, budget-bound, online-only, or pod-bound in ways generic advice does not handle.

# Vocabulary and Classification Problems

- “Improved” means different things: stronger, smoother, more fun, more creative, cheaper, less salty, more social, or more personally authored.
- “Veggies” is not universally understood and carries conflicting meanings.
- “Draw” can mean card advantage, card selection, filtering, cantrips, or simply seeing more cards.
- “Bracket” blends power, card restrictions, play patterns, social permission, and emotional comfort.
- “Casual” does not mean unwilling to win; some players optimize within casual constraints.
- “Advice” includes direct card changes, tools, theory, social norms, negative examples, and disagreement.

# Beginner and Accessibility Evidence

- New players use videos to learn hidden Commander norms, not only card mechanics.
- Scryfall is powerful but intimidating when syntax feels like coding.
- Returning players from older formats may need basic Commander deck-construction scaffolds.
- Beginners benefit from general heuristics, but those heuristics can become restrictive later.
- Videos accelerate card-pool exposure for players who cannot know decades of cards.
- Advice overload can make Commander feel harder rather than easier.

# Social and Contextual Evidence

- Pod norms, online tables, LGS reputation, and closed metas materially change whether advice works.
- Players modify decks to avoid archenemy status, arms races, or table resentment.
- Some players build for entertainment value and opponent experience.
- Other players reject salt avoidance and view hate, stax, and slowdown as normal game friction.
- One player’s deck changes can influence an entire group’s interaction, redundancy, and power expectations.
- Content can help players understand why other people build differently.

# Building Versus Playing

- Some players enjoy deep theory, formulas, calculators, and structured deckbuilding.
- Others need real games before theory matters.
- Some players love the deckbuilding process; others dislike building and prefer playing.
- A deck can be easy to explain but hard to pilot.
- Deck construction skill, gameplay sequencing, politics, and threat assessment are separate competencies.
- Players may build from owned cards, bulk, budget, or bracket constraints rather than from ideal singles.

# Current Versus Desired Experience

- Players move from copying lists toward personal discovery.
- Players move from commander-centric builds toward strategy-centric shells.
- Players move from low-resource decks toward higher land/draw consistency.
- Players move from generic staples toward tailored role cards.
- Some players want to power up within a bracket; others want to power down without making junk.
- Some players want permission to build less optimal, more expressive decks.

# Final Retained Findings

1. Scryfall literacy is one of the strongest improvement signals because it creates discovery and ownership.

12. Overreliance on the commander is linked to fragility, boredom, and table ire.
13. Beginner players use videos to learn Commander’s hidden norms and huge card pool.
14. Veteran players often use content for reinforcement, card discovery, or disagreement rather than direct instruction.
15. Advice can improve decks, worsen social fit, or increase anxiety depending on context.
16. Pod, shop, online, budget, and owned-card constraints materially change what advice works.
17. Bracket language is not a simple casual-to-competitive scale.
18. Players often want strong decks inside chosen constraints rather than maximum power.
19. Real games remain the highest-trust validation source.
20. The source strongly supports separating deck behavior from player motivation.
