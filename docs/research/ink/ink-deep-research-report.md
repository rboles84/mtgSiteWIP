# Ink and Altruism

## Executive Summary

- **RGWU is best understood as a blackless coalition, not as a vague “all the good colors” blend.** In Commander 2016 design, Wizards explicitly framed RGWU as **“Altruism,”** describing red as loving its friends, green as valuing symbiosis, white as prioritizing community, and blue as treating knowledge as something to share; the excluded color, black, is framed as seeing selfishness as a virtue. That missing-color pressure is the core of the identity. citeturn55view0turn40view2

- **Philosophically, Ink / Altruism is a worldview of public flourishing.** White contributes the moral claim that suffering should be reduced through collective order; blue contributes learning and planned improvement; red contributes emotional openness, loyalty, and action; green contributes interdependence, continuity, and organic growth. Remove black’s self-prioritization and power-seeking, and the resulting ethos leans toward shared prosperity, open teaching, anti-hoarding norms, and the idea that abundance is healthiest when circulated rather than captured. citeturn40view0turn42view0turn41view4turn41view2turn40view2turn55view0

- **Mechanically, the clearest official expression of RGWU is political generosity defended by soft control.** Kynaios and Tiro of Meletis draw their controller a card at end step, let every player try an extra land drop, and compensate only the opponents who decline with a card; the original Stalwart Unity precon backed that up with Howling Mine, Temple Bell, Rites of Flourishing, Minds Aglow, Collective Voyage, Ghostly Prison, Propaganda, and Sphere of Safety. That is not naïve charity. It is generosity shaped into leverage. citeturn16search0turn43view0

- **“Ink” is not an official Wizards faction name.** It is a community shorthand derived from **Ink-Treader Nephilim**, one of Guildpact’s first-ever four-color cards. Wizards still had not standardized official four-color combination names as of Mark Rosewater’s May 2024 comment that he assumed they would give the combinations names “one day,” while third-party Commander resources still use Ink-Treader/Ink labels in practice. citeturn25search6turn26search2turn51search9

- **The current RGWU card evidence is small but coherent.** The directly verified, currently released paper cards with exact RGWU identity for this packet are Ink-Treader Nephilim, Kynaios and Tiro of Meletis, Omnath, Locus of Creation, Aragorn, the Uniter, and The Fourteenth Doctor. By contrast, cards that merely *look* RGWU from their mana cost can fail the test of Commander color identity; notably, Avatar Aang’s reverse-face rules text contains all five mana symbols, making it five-color rather than RGWU. citeturn24search1turn16search0turn54search0turn50search2turn37search1turn35view2turn19search0

- **Four-color design is rare because it needs a reason not to be five-color.** Wizards has repeatedly said four-color cards are difficult because they can become unfocused “soup” unless the absent color supplies the identity anchor. That is exactly why blacklessness matters so much to RGWU: the combination becomes most legible when it is framed against what it refuses to do. citeturn25search3turn55view0

## Identity Thesis

**Ink / Altruism is the civic, generous, knowledge-sharing, growth-oriented coalition of red, green, white, and blue: a philosophy that believes emotion, wisdom, structure, and learning reach their highest form when they are distributed across a community rather than concentrated into private power.** citeturn55view0turn40view0turn42view0turn41view4turn41view2

Its defining pressure is the **absence of black**. Without black, RGWU loses the color most committed to private ambition, ruthless advancement, instrumental exploitation, and the moral legitimacy of putting one’s own gain first; what remains is a coalition that still wants to build, improve, feel, and flourish, but prefers those goods to be **mutual** rather than extractive. That blackless condition is what turns mere multicolor abundance into something like public abundance. citeturn40view2turn55view0

This makes RGWU strategically more interesting than “nice people tribal.” Its signature move is not simple generosity. It is **structured generosity**: giving cards, mana, land drops, breathing room, and table stability in ways that align everyone’s incentives with your own long game. In Commander terms, Ink / Altruism often wants the table to develop, but on terms that leave the Ink player safest, best-informed, and best-positioned to convert shared growth into a decisive late-game edge. citeturn16search0turn43view0turn31search0turn36search9

## Historical Basis

Guildpact’s Nephilim cycle was Magic’s first four-color cycle. Mark Rosewater later explained that the Nephilim were made in Guildpact because the team was asked to create something for players who might not enjoy the guild structure; in a multicolor block, they used that opportunity to make four-color creatures for the first time. He also later identified the Nephilim cycle as the first four-color cards in the game and acknowledged that, in hindsight, they should have been legendary. citeturn25search0turn25search6turn25search3

Within that cycle, **Ink-Treader Nephilim** is the blackless entry. Its Oracle text makes any instant or sorcery that targets only it get copied for every other creature that spell could target, and Scryfall’s rulings note that this effect is mandatory. Mechanically, that means one private instruction becomes a public event. Flavor-wise, Wizards never published an official philosophical essay for the card’s name itself, so any symbolic reading of “ink” is interpretive. Still, the combination of the name **Ink-Treader** and the card’s mass-propagation mechanic makes the player shorthand unusually resonant: ink marks, records, and reproduces; the card turns one line of spell text into a table-wide script. citeturn24search0turn24search1turn17search3

Commander 2016 is the real foundation for RGWU’s modern identity. Ethan Fleischer’s design article states that the team eventually stopped treating four-color design as a loose mechanical collage and instead tried to give each four-color group a more coherent faction-like identity defined by its missing color. In that framework, RGWU became **“Altruism.”** Fleischer’s summary is the cleanest official statement Wizards has ever published for the combination: red loves its friends, green values symbiosis, white values community, blue believes knowledge should be shared, and black sees selfishness as a virtue. The official deck expression of that idea was **Stalwart Unity**, fronted by **Kynaios and Tiro of Meletis**. citeturn55view0turn43view0

Commander 2016 also explains the broader four-color naming problem. Wizards gave the decks theme labels—**Artifice, Chaos, Aggression, Altruism, Growth**—but Rosewater later said he assumed Wizards would give four-color combinations names “one day,” which strongly implies there still was no universal official naming system in place as of 2024. In practice, players therefore draw from two naming pools: the Nephilim names from Guildpact and the Commander 2016 theme labels. That is why “Ink” and “Altruism” coexist, but neither should be treated as a formal in-game rules term. citeturn55view0turn26search2

After Commander 2016, later RGWU-adjacent representatives broadened the identity without replacing its blackless core. **Omnath, Locus of Creation** expresses abundance through escalating landfall rewards. **Aragorn, the Uniter** turns each color in a spell into a different public-facing payoff, rewarding coalition casting rather than mono-vector efficiency. **The Fourteenth Doctor** was explicitly designed to support Doctor’s companion deck building, using hybrid mana to reach RGWU identity. These later cards do not all play group hug, but together they show a broader through-line: RGWU is good at turning plurality into coherence. citeturn54search0turn50search2turn33search0turn37search1

A necessary correction for this dossier: **Avatar Aang is not an RGWU card for Commander identity purposes.** Its front face costs RGWU, but its transformed face contains a cost-reduction ability with **{W}{U}{B}{R}{G}**, and Commander color identity counts mana symbols from the whole card, including rules text. So although Avatar Aang looks blackless at first glance, it is functionally five-color for Commander construction. citeturn35view2turn19search0

## Included Color Contributions and Excluded Color Analysis

White gives RGWU its **moral center**. White’s goal is peace, and Rosewater describes white as believing the world has enough resources to reduce unnecessary suffering if individuals prioritize the good of the group over their own immediate wants. White also supplies the preference for structure, law, protection, and the scaling power of many small pieces acting together. In Ink / Altruism, white is the part that says generosity should not be random; it should be built into the system. citeturn40view0turn42view2

Blue gives RGWU its **educational and infrastructural intelligence**. Blue wants perfection through knowledge, study, tools, and systems; Rosewater even singles out universities, libraries, and laboratories as quintessential blue institutions. In isolation, blue often wants that knowledge for leverage. Inside RGWU’s official Altruism frame, however, blue is redirected: the knowledge it values is treated as something that ought to circulate. In other words, blue provides the archive, the curriculum, the planning horizon, and the informational elegance. Black’s absence pressures blue away from proprietary optimization and toward public instruction. citeturn42view0turn55view0

Red gives RGWU its **heart and immediacy**. Red wants freedom, lives through feeling, acts in the moment, and values loyalty, camaraderie, and fierce personal bonds. That matters because a purely white-blue-green coalition could become sterile, dutiful, or over-managed. Red is what warms the public square. It makes the identity hospitable, festive, relational, and willing to act before a perfect consensus document has been drafted. In Ink / Altruism, red is also why generosity feels like welcome rather than mere administration. citeturn41view0turn41view4

Green gives RGWU its **ecology of belonging**. Green wants acceptance rather than conquest, sees life as an interconnected web, and places high value on community, interdependence, ancestry, tradition, and wisdom transmitted over time. That contribution is crucial to the “public abundance” reading of RGWU: prosperity is not just redistributed wealth, but a living system in which growth is healthiest when its nutrients keep moving. Green is why Ink / Altruism does not stop at policy and information; it also cares about habitat, seasonality, inheritance, and continuity. citeturn41view2turn41view3

Black would normally contribute **ambition, self-protection, realism, opportunism, and the willingness to use any means necessary to achieve one’s ends**. Rosewater characterizes black as wanting power, treating self-interest as the most reliable political fact, and refusing moral or social constraints when they obstruct success. Mechanically and philosophically, black is the color most willing to sacrifice others for personal advancement, hoard advantage, or weaponize asymmetry without apology. citeturn40view2turn42view3turn42view4

Its absence creates the real center of gravity for RGWU. Without black, the coalition loses the color most prepared to say, “I deserve more than you,” or “I will take this because I can.” What emerges is not harmlessness, but a different theory of advantage: benefit should be **shared, socialized, and legitimized in public**, even when the Ink player quietly positions themselves to profit most from that sharing. This blackless condition produces radical cooperation, selfless teaching, defensive diplomacy, anti-hoarding aesthetics, and a suspicion of prosperity that is privately captured rather than collectively felt. At its best, that is communal flourishing; at its worst, it becomes paternalism, performative benevolence, or endless consensus without decisive closure. citeturn55view0turn40view2turn42view2turn42view1turn41view4

## Mechanical Identity

The most concrete official mechanical pattern for RGWU is **group hug under protective cover**. Kynaios and Tiro’s own text gives the controller a guaranteed card, then helps every player make an extra land drop, with a fallback card only for opponents who cannot or choose not to deploy a land. The Stalwart Unity deck amplified that identity with Howling Mine, Temple Bell, Rites of Flourishing, Minds Aglow, Collective Voyage, Tempt with Discovery, and other cards that enlarge the whole table’s access to resources. This is the strongest evidence that Wizards intended RGWU to feel like communal acceleration rather than private extraction. citeturn16search0turn43view0

Just as important, that generosity was paired with **political defense and pillow forting**. Stalwart Unity shipped with Ghostly Prison, Propaganda, Sphere of Safety, Windborn Muse, Kazuul, Tyrant of the Cliffs, and other effects that make attacking the “helpful” player inconvenient. Ethan Fleischer’s deck description says the Altruism deck gives out mana, cards, and tokens, but must also include tools that make attacking its pilot difficult. That pairing is the mechanical heart of Ink / Altruism: benevolence is not surrender; it is protected soft power. citeturn43view0turn55view0

That soft power is usually **asymmetrical in practice even when symmetrical in appearance**. Kynaios and Tiro always draw their controller a card; opponents only draw if they fail to convert the offered land-drop opportunity. Group-hug cards often make the entire table stronger, but the Ink player normally builds to capitalize on that strength better than everyone else—through better mana sinks, stronger pillow-fort protection, more efficient card conversion, or better scaling payoffs. Community deck discourse around Kynaios and Tiro repeatedly describes the commander as helping everyone’s decks function while also making the pilot a surprisingly unattractive removal target. citeturn16search0turn36search9

Beyond Kynaios and Tiro, RGWU also supports **shared-growth and coalition-value** play. Omnath, Locus of Creation is not a group-hug card, but it is a textbook RGWU abundance engine: card draw on entry, then layered landfall that turns more land drops into life, mana, and eventually pressure on opponents. Aragorn, the Uniter likewise rewards the full coalition by turning each color’s contribution into a different payoff, and official release notes confirm multicolored spells can trigger multiple abilities. These cards show that blackless abundance can also be expressed as integrated value rather than open-handed gifting. citeturn54search0turn50search2

RGWU also has a smaller but thematically important line in **spell reflection and shared targeting**. Ink-Treader Nephilim is the most literal example: a spell aimed at one creature becomes a spell written across all valid creatures. That is not a broad archetype by card count, but it is an unusually powerful symbolic anchor for the “Ink” label because it turns private address into public inscription. In Commander, that often translates into copy chains, mass cantrip-spreads, table-wide tricks, or outsized tactical swings built around target-sharing. citeturn24search0turn17search3

Community metagame data reinforces the central archetype. On EDHREC, Kynaios and Tiro is most strongly associated with **Group Hug**, with adjacent tags for **Wheels**, **Lands Matter**, **Pillow Fort**, and **Politics**. Omnath’s page clusters around **Landfall**, **Lands Matter**, and **Ramp**. Aragorn’s page clusters around **Multicolor Matters**, with adjacent **Legends**, **Tokens**, and **Humans** support. The Fourteenth Doctor’s Commander pages lean toward **Doctors**, **Historic**, and **Legends**, with commander usage often improved by picking a partner such as Clara Oswald. This spread matters because it shows the difference between **the official identity thesis** of RGWU and **the wider mechanical envelope** players currently build within it. citeturn31search0turn31search2turn31search4turn31search6turn31search8turn32search0turn32search1turn32search2turn32search6

The emotional experience of playing Ink / Altruism in Commander is therefore distinctive. You often feel like the table’s host, not its tyrant: smoothing mana, increasing velocity, discouraging early hostility, and inviting everyone to “play real Magic.” The table perception that follows is equally distinctive: opponents are often reluctant to attack the player who is drawing them cards or enabling extra land drops, especially if attacking also means paying through Ghostly Prison or Propaganda. The danger for the pod is that this social legitimacy can let the Ink player build the best endgame in plain sight. citeturn43view0turn36search9

## Philosophical Identity and Creative Direction

**Values.** Ink / Altruism values mutual uplift, public learning, patient institution-building, ecological reciprocity, and emotionally sincere cooperation. It assumes that the world gets better when people share tools, information, and material support instead of bottling them up for private leverage. It prefers libraries to vaults, gardens to stockpiles, and festivals to courts. That value mix follows directly from white’s peace-through-collectivity, blue’s improvement-through-knowledge, red’s relational passion, green’s interdependence, and the Commander 2016 Altruism brief. citeturn40view0turn42view0turn41view4turn41view2turn55view0

**Fears.** Ink fears hoarding, cynicism, atomization, and coercive hierarchy—but it also fears collapse into chaos. White and blue still want systems; green still wants continuity; red still wants people to feel fully alive. So Ink is not anarchic generosity. It is generosity with architecture. What it dreads most is a world where power is privatized, trust is impossible, and every exchange becomes an extraction. citeturn40view0turn42view0turn40view2

**Strengths.** Its strengths are social legitimacy, coalition play, incremental scaling, and broad-spectrum answers. White gives coordination and defense; blue gives planning and information; red gives speed of feeling and willingness to act; green gives resilience and natural growth. In gameplay, that often means Ink can do a little of everything while making those actions feel socially acceptable. In creative terms, it produces societies centered on open archives, civic rituals, seasonal abundance, apprenticeship, and coordinated festivals. citeturn42view2turn42view1turn41view4turn41view3turn43view0

**Failure modes.** Its biggest failures are overextension, paternalism, indecision, and gift-wrapped manipulation. White can become rigid and dismiss the individual; blue can become passive and over-analytical; red can become short-sighted; green can romanticize what already exists. Without black’s instinct for hard prioritization and self-preservation, the coalition can also drift into kingmaking, moral vanity, or a refusal to draw necessary lines until too late. citeturn42view2turn42view1turn41view4turn41view2turn40view2

**Internal contradictions.** RGWU is not naturally serene. White and blue like plans; red resists overplanning. Blue wants improvement; green is suspicious of needless change. White wants order; red wants freedom. Ink works only when these tensions are resolved through reciprocal institutions: places where knowledge is shared rather than weaponized, tradition is living rather than stagnant, and emotion is welcomed without being allowed to burn the whole structure down. That is why “river monasteries,” “public archives,” and “festival tables” fit the identity so well. They reconcile order, learning, feeling, and growth without resorting to black’s private ambition. citeturn40view0turn42view0turn41view4turn41view2

From that philosophical base, the strongest **visual motifs** for a Vox Mana treatment are shared meals under long canopies, open libraries with hand-copied maps and scrolls, civic observatories, communal gardens irrigated by channels of colored ink, public seed vaults, schoolrooms lit like scriptoria, and treaty halls where records are preserved for descendants rather than for state secrecy. Blue’s libraries and laboratories, white’s institutions, green’s webs of interdependence, and red’s celebratory social energy all point in this direction. citeturn42view0turn40view0turn41view3turn41view4

The strongest **environmental motifs** are places where knowledge and nourishment literally circulate: river monasteries, terraced gardens, canal-cities, archive cloisters, civic granaries, and mirrored pools whose surfaces hold texts or constellations. Ink as a symbolic layer works especially well when it is shown not as secretive black script, but as public mark-making—lesson diagrams, copied maps, irrigation plans, treaty signatures, and communal memory carried forward by hand. That reading is interpretive, but it is strongly suggested by the name-and-mechanic pairing of Ink-Treader Nephilim and by RGWU’s official alignment with shared resources and shared knowledge. citeturn24search0turn55view0

The strongest **character and society motifs** are teachers and apprentices, scholar-builders, celebrants, stewards, gardeners, archivists, astronomers, negotiators, and civic hosts. The society should feel open-handed and infrastructural rather than imperial. What it should **avoid** are motifs that feel too black: private vaults, throne rooms built around fear, blood-price economies, predatory patronage, conspicuous luxury as a status weapon, or archives restricted to elite initiates. Ink should feel like wisdom that belongs to a people, not information trapped inside a ruler. citeturn40view2turn55view0

For **website hero backgrounds**, three directions fit especially well. One is a riverside archive where illuminated manuscripts, star charts, and canal-water all flow in the same visual rhythm. Another is a communal harvest table running through a public courtyard, ringed by teachers, musicians, gardeners, and mapmakers. A third is a civic observatory whose reflecting pools dissolve individual silhouettes into layered constellations and handwritten diagrams—identity submerged into shared memory, without ever feeling sinister. citeturn42view0turn41view3turn41view4turn55view0

## Comparison to Other Four-Color Identities and Vox Mana Dossier Draft

Because Wizards still has not standardized permanent official names for four-color combinations, the contrasts below use the familiar community shorthand from Nephilim-derived labels and the official Commander 2016 theme labels only as descriptive aids, not as sanctioned rules vocabulary. citeturn26search2turn55view0

**Against WUBR and Artifice.** Artifice is everything except green. Ethan Fleischer described it as the artifact-focused coalition of civilization, technology, artificiality, and creative destruction. Ink differs by distrusting the break with living systems. Where Artifice builds machines and treats the natural/artificial boundary as morally negotiable, Ink wants systems that still feel ecological, humane, and communally embedded. citeturn55view0

**Against UBRG and Chaos.** Chaos is everything except white. It embraces variance, experiments, impulsive unfolding, and resistance to imposed order. Ink differs by wanting public order and hospitality. Both can be exuberant and anti-stagnant, but Chaos rejects formal civic containment, while Ink wants a structure generous enough to hold plurality without dissolving into disorder. citeturn55view0

**Against BRGW and Aggression.** Aggression is everything except blue. It fights first, hates durdling, and prioritizes survival, pressure, and combat resolution over reflection. Ink differs by insisting that knowledge and planning matter, and by treating cooperation as a strategic engine rather than wasted tempo. Aggression wants to end the argument; Ink wants to shape the whole table’s conditions of play before turning the corner. citeturn55view0

**Against GWUB and Growth.** Growth is everything except red. It is patient, scaling, counters-heavy, and future-oriented, but it lacks red’s warmth, improvisation, and celebratory immediacy. Ink differs by making public life feel lived rather than merely cultivated. Growth accumulates; Ink circulates. Growth is the orchard; Ink is the orchard’s harvest feast, schoolhouse, and waterworks. citeturn55view0turn41view4

**One-sentence identity line.**
Ink / Altruism is the blackless politics of mutual flourishing: emotion, knowledge, order, and growth devoted to goods meant to be shared. citeturn55view0turn40view2

**Worldview.**
This identity believes the highest form of power is not domination but circulation. Knowledge should be taught, prosperity should be distributed, institutions should reduce suffering, and communities should be designed so that one person’s flourishing strengthens everyone else’s. It is not blind to strategy; it simply rejects the idea that strategy must culminate in hoarding, coercion, or private advancement for its own sake. Where black would secure the self first and justify the rest later, Ink starts from reciprocity and tries to make the self thrive *through* the commons rather than against it. citeturn40view0turn42view0turn41view2turn41view4turn40view2turn55view0

**Play pattern.**
As a Commander identity, Ink behaves like a benevolent architect. It accelerates mana, widens access to cards, lowers the social temperature, and lets the table feel productive. In the same motion, it layers defensive friction, political goodwill, and scaling engines that let the pilot profit more from the shared expansion than anyone else. Its ideal win is not an abrupt mugging after false friendliness; it is the moment when the whole table’s increased development has, almost imperceptibly, created a board state that favors the Ink player most. citeturn16search0turn43view0turn31search0turn36search9

**Visual identity.**
Visually, Ink should look like public memory made beautiful: illuminated classrooms, canal-fed gardens, treaty halls, city observatories, hand-copied maps, festival tables, and mirror pools inscribed with lessons for future generations. Its palette should feel sunlit and civic rather than courtly or clandestine. The operative image is not the secret notebook, but the open archive: inscriptions that exist so others can read them. citeturn42view0turn41view3turn55view0turn24search0

**Keyword tags.**
Public abundance; shared knowledge; civic reciprocity; protected generosity; coalition value.

**What this identity is not.**
It is not ruthless ambition.
It is not private hoarding.
It is not sentiment without structure.
It is not sterile bureaucracy without warmth.
It is not naïve gifting that ignores asymmetry.
