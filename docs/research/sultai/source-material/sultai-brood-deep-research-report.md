## Confirmed Canon

Sultai is one of Tarkir’s **five clans**, and Wizards’ official naming article identifies it as the **black-green-blue** combination. Official Tarkir worldbuilding further identifies the Sultai Brood as the clan of **ruthlessness**, symbolized by the dragon’s **fang**. In other words, “Sultai” is not merely a Commander color shorthand in origin; it is first a specific Tarkiri political and cultural faction. citeturn30view0turn10view0turn13view0

In the **Khans of Tarkir** present, Wizards’ primary world guide describes the Sultai as having become “wealthy and powerful by exploitation of the people and the resources of the land.” Their chief instrument of power is **necromancy**, and their undead labor force—called **sibsig**—is used to mine, farm, and dredge rivers and seas for food and dragon bone. The same guide also states that the Sultai were the first clan to bargain with **rakshasas**, and that the clan is ruled by a **lineage of naga**. citeturn10view0

That same Khans guide confirms the internal social makeup so often associated with Sultai: **naga**, **humans**, **rakshasas**, **zombies**, and mixed undead armies drawn from many clans and species. It explicitly says that mages are naga, human, and rakshasa; that **human merchants called the Panjasi** are the traders and dealers of the Sultai; and that **zombies are servants throughout the Sultai**. It also confirms that the upper-class naga treat human dead as **chattel** and call the making of undead “the second skin.” citeturn11view0

The Khans guide also confirms the palace-and-jungle image: **Sidisi** rules from **Kheru Temple** and courts at **Qarsi Palace**, where luxury barges and “legendary luxuries” are part of her political theater. It names important Sultai locations including **Ukud Necropolis**, **Gudul**, **Gurmag Swamps**, **Marang River Fortress**, **the Sagu**, **Molderfang Falls**, and the **Crocodile Pits**. Those place names are canon-supported, not wiki-only. citeturn11view0

In the **Fate Reforged** past, official worldbuilding preserves the same broad image of decadence, cruelty, undead labor, and treachery, but with an important difference in emphasis. The guide says the clan’s rulers made pacts with rakshasas to secure elite status, while also stating that although naga command respect as necromancers, they do **not dominate** the clan. It presents **Tasigur, the Golden Fang** as the “young, pampered heir to the Sultai fortune,” surrounded by zombie bearers and refined cruelty, and it describes the clan defending itself from dragons through poisoned bolts, stealth, zombie lookouts, and jungle wards. citeturn35view0turn35view1turn35view4

By the official story **“Khanfall,”** however, Tasigur is no longer merely heir: he is explicitly called the **khan** of the Sultai at the summit of clan leaders, and he betrays that summit to the dragons. This matters because it separates the question “what was the Sultai in Tarkir’s ancient past?” from the narrower question “who led them immediately before the dragonlords won?” The answer to the latter is clearly **Tasigur**, not Sidisi. citeturn36view0

In the altered present of **Dragons of Tarkir**, the relevant faction is no longer the Sultai Brood but the **Silumgar clan**. Wizards’ official guide states that when undead are counted, Silumgar’s clan is numerically unrivaled; that it embodies the draconic aspect of **ruthlessness**; and that its hierarchy is **top-down**, with **Silumgar first**, then dragons, then human priests, then warriors, then servants and zombies. The guide also states that the **extinct Sultai** once used corpses for labor and war, and that Silumgar’s clan continues those necromantic practices. citeturn12view0

The changed status of **Sidisi** is especially important. Official story summaries and the Dragons guide say Sidisi is **no longer khan and no longer living** in that timeline. Silumgar had her executed for manipulation, then raised her as an intelligent undead servant—**Sidisi, Undead Vizier**—whom he could more closely control. She retains influence, but it is influence **under** dragon rule, not the sovereign authority she held in Khans-era canon. citeturn12view0turn13view0turn29search2

Official Wizards summary material also makes the timeline boundary explicit: the original five clans from Khans **did exist** in Tarkir’s past, but in the dragonlord timeline their names and histories were largely stamped out. That is the cleanest official reason not to collapse “Sultai Brood” and “Silumgar clan” into one undifferentiated thing. citeturn13view0

A later official return to Tarkir in **Tarkir: Dragonstorm** introduces yet another distinct state: a **reformed Sultai** that has defeated Silumgar, decentralized leadership, honored certain undead as continuing civic authorities, and rejected rakshasa pacts rather than embracing them. That later material is canon, but it is not evidence that the original Khans-era Sultai were decentralized, humane, or anti-rakshasa. citeturn42view0turn29search3

## Design Commentary and Color Philosophy

From the standpoint of official design commentary, original Khans-era Sultai was very clearly the **base-black** clan. Mark Rosewater’s released design handoff document states: “**Sultai, Clan of Ruthlessness**,” with **base color: black**, **supporting colors: blue and green**, and **key mechanic: delve**. That is the strongest direct answer to the question “what does black-centered mean for Sultai?” in design terms. citeturn7view2

Rosewater’s Sultai-week design article then fleshes out the philosophy. In that piece, the three Sultai colors introduce themselves as **Black: power through opportunity**, **Green: acceptance through wisdom**, and **Blue: perfection through knowledge**. Later in the same article, Rosewater’s color-voices agree that Sultai-style ruthlessness is fundamentally about being willing to do what needs to be done, and they describe the faction in general as the set of colors willing to **cut off resources, steal things, and “use the dead.”** That is design commentary, not story text, but it is the clearest official synthesis of Sultai’s intended worldview. citeturn7view0turn33view3turn33view5

So what does **black-centered** mean here in philosophical rather than purely mechanical terms? It means that the clan’s primary impulse is **ambition, self-interest, and instrumental power**. Black’s own official “My Words” article defines its method as inventorying every available resource, being “creative and ruthless,” and doing “whatever it takes” to seize control of one’s destiny. In Sultai, that black core does not stand alone: it is reinforced by blue’s appetite for knowledge, calculation, manipulation, and preparation, and by green’s comfort with harsh natural systems, predation, adaptation, burial, regrowth, and long timelines. citeturn32view0turn32view1turn32view2turn33view4

What **green** adds to Sultai is not softness. Official green philosophy stresses that everything belongs to an interconnected ecosystem, that every thing has a role, and that life and death are embedded in food-web relationships and slow, purposeful adaptation. In the Sultai interview, green is also the color that can frame harshness as part of the world’s real order rather than an aberration. That helps explain why Sultai’s necromancy is not merely “black plus zombies”; it can be read, in color-pie terms, as black ambition operating within a worldview that sees nothing as outside the cycle of use, decay, and replacement. citeturn32view2turn33view2

What **blue** adds is planning, asymmetry, and refinement. Official blue philosophy begins from the idea that identity is not fixed, that people are shaped through knowledge and tools, and that information is the route to better outcomes. In the Sultai interview, blue explicitly says it wants a plan, wants to know what it is up against, and wants to prepare. That is the part of Sultai that makes its cruelty feel calculated rather than merely frenzied. citeturn32view1turn33view4

The absence of **white** is one of the most important structuring absences in Sultai, but it has to be handled carefully. Wizards does not publish a single canonical sentence saying “Sultai lacks white, therefore it lacks justice,” yet official color-pie material gives that reading strong support. White’s official philosophy centers the **needs of the group** over individual desire and imagines a world ordered to reduce suffering for all. Black’s own article mocks white’s morality and “good of the group” framing, and Khans-era Sultai canon shows societies built around exploitation, class hierarchy, luxury for the few, and instrumental use of the dead. Taken together, those official materials strongly support the claim that Sultai is not animated by equality, shared obligation, or altruistic order. citeturn32view3turn32view0turn10view0turn11view0

The absence of **red** is different. Red’s official philosophy values listening to inner feeling, acting authentically, accepting messiness, and resisting oppressive rule-making. In the Sultai interview, blue frames its alliance with black as more prepared and ordered than red, while also complaining that black is too willing to risk and cross lines. That means Sultai is not best understood as a freedom-seeking, spontaneous, expressive faction. Its magic and politics are more often rendered as **premeditated**, **patient**, **venomous**, **opportunistic**, and **hierarchical**. I would therefore describe the “absence of red” in Sultai as an absence of freedom-first, impulse-first legitimacy—not an absence of desire, because black certainly has desire, but an absence of red’s belief that desire itself is a guide to a good life. citeturn32view4turn33view4turn8view5

That is why reducing Sultai to “villain colors” is too shallow. The official evidence points to a more coherent worldview: power is real, hierarchy is real, predation is real, death is usable, knowledge is leverage, and scruples that get in the way of survival or dominance are liabilities. That is a philosophy, not just a mood. citeturn7view2turn33view3turn32view0turn32view2

## Culture, Society, and Historical Change

The **Khans-era** Sultai culture in official worldbuilding is one of the clearest faction identities Wizards has ever published. The clan inhabits **city-temples, necropoleis, and pleasure palaces** in a tropical river-delta jungle. **Qarsi Palace** is a “sprawling, luxurious jungle paradise” of waterways, paper lanterns, barges, and pleasure domains; **Kheru Temple** is both cult center and seat of rule; **Ukud Necropolis** is a monumental tomb complex and site of darker rituals; **Gudul**, **Gurmag**, **Marang River Fortress**, and **the Sagu** complete a domain defined by lush danger, waterways, and undead-infested borders. citeturn11view0

The social structure is just as explicit. The clan is ruled, in the Khans present, by a **lineage of naga**, but its working reality is multi-species: naga, humans, rakshasas, zombies, and mixed undead. The guide assigns specific roles—mages at Kheru or Ukud, warriors and assassins in Gurmag/Marang/Sagu, **Panjasi** merchants as traders and killers, and zombies as servants. This is not merely “a zombie faction”; it is a **courtly exploitation state** built on living specialists and dead labor. citeturn10view0turn11view0

Its magical culture is likewise broader than reanimation alone. Official material describes the Sultai use of **body parts in ritual magic**, **hypnosis and charm**, the deadly **lotus perfume**, naga **body-warping** and necromantic modification, and venoms powerful enough to kill with a pinprick. Human Sultai mages practice elementalism and shamanism, partly to survive within the tyrannical order above them, while rakshasas handle the most land-ruining, spirit-heavy, catastrophic dark magic. Poison, mind control, and ritualized corpse-use are therefore all canon-supported features of Sultai culture. citeturn11view0

The official picture of **Sidisi** fits this society exactly. The Khans guide calls her both **queen** and **khan**, says her power comes from wealth plus rakshasa-taught magic, and portrays her as haughty, cruel, crafty, and ready to slaughter opposition. Her stated imperial ambition is also explicit: she seeks to reunite Tarkir’s clans into a single empire under **her** banner. Sidisi is therefore not just a local tyrant; she is an expansionist ruler whose empire already rests on extraction, spectacle, and necromantic logistics. citeturn11view0

The **Fate Reforged** Sultai shows continuity and change. Continuity: the clan is still decadent, treacherous, undead-heavy, and dragon-fang ruthless; it still fields poisoned defenses and uses zombies as disposable labor and military fodder. Change: the official guide shifts emphasis away from a plainly naga-dominated monarchy and toward a broader decaying aristocracy in which naga are feared necromancers but not total monopolists on power. **Tasigur** embodies this version well: a pampered, cruel aristocratic heir whose luxury is literally carried by the dead. citeturn35view0turn35view1turn35view4

By **late Fate Reforged**, the leadership question settles differently again: “Khanfall” confirms Tasigur as **khan** during the dragon crisis, and his betrayal sets up the clan’s fall into dragonlord domination. That moment matters because it means “historical Sultai” is not one static constitution. The clan persists across centuries, but its ruling arrangements shift from ancient aristocratic pact-making to Tasigur’s late-khan leadership and finally to its absorption under Silumgar. citeturn36view0

The **Dragons of Tarkir** Silumgar clan preserves many Sultai textures while changing the core of authority. The official guide describes Silumgar’s court as a place of **capricious favor**, **assassination**, **extortion**, and sycophantic translation. Humans can rise high but only precariously; naga largely lose real power except through Sidisi; sibsig remain essential; Qarsi Palace remains luxurious; Ukud becomes partly a dragon tomb-vault; and the same swamp/jungle geography still structures the region. This is why Silumgar’s brood can feel Sultai-like without being identical to the original Sultai Brood. citeturn12view0

A final complication comes from **Tarkir: Dragonstorm**, which depicts a post-Silumgar **reformed Sultai** rather than either prior model. That official guide describes decentralized governance, undead leaders honored for their expertise, agriculture and trade as civic foundations, spies and scouts as an information network, naga protectors, and strong hostility to rakshasa pacts. This later Sultai still uses necromancy and still values elegance, trade, espionage, and the principle that “nothing goes to waste,” but it is explicitly a response against both Silumgar and the “old Sultai” elite order. citeturn42view0

## Mechanics, Cards, and Gameplay Identity

Mechanically, original Sultai is inseparable from **delve**. Wizards’ official mechanics article for *Fate Reforged* says the Sultai have been “using every resource available to them since time immemorial,” identify with **ruthlessness**, and use delve as a way to fuel powerful spells with the dead. The *Khans of Tarkir* mechanics article adds the operative rules point: delve can pay only for **generic** mana, not colored mana, and exiled cards stay exiled even if the spell is countered. citeturn15view1turn15view0

Design commentary makes the flavor fit explicit. Rosewater’s handoff document says delve was a “perfect fit” because Sultai uses the undead as a resource, and because the clan was designed for **slower gameplay**. His later *Dragons* design article also explains that when the team looked for a mechanic to succeed Sultai’s delve in the black-blue Silumgar transition, they first explored **self-mill**, then discard, then graveyard-from-battlefield movement, eventually landing on **exploit**. That sequence is revealing: official design consistently associates Sultai/Silumgar space with graveyard conversion, sacrifice, attrition, and using dead matter as a second economy. citeturn7view2turn7view3

The card file reinforces that identity. **Sidisi, Brood Tyrant** mills three when entering or attacking and makes Zombies whenever creature cards are milled from your library. **Sultai Soothsayer** looks at the top four, puts one into hand, and dumps the rest into the graveyard. **Sultai Ascendancy** effectively turns each upkeep into controlled graveyard setup. **Tasigur, the Golden Fang** has delve and a recursion engine; **Necropolis Fiend** uses delve and further graveyard exiling as removal; **Dead Drop** turns graveyard stockpiles into edict-style control; **Empty the Pits** turns that same stockpile into a Zombie army; and **Treasure Cruise** turns it into raw card advantage. Those are not all “about reanimation,” but they are all about **resource conversion through the graveyard**. citeturn21search0turn21search4turn21search5turn22search1turn22search2turn22search9turn17search1turn22search25turn34search0turn34search1turn34search3

That helps clarify an important distinction. **Tarkir/Sultai canon** is not “all BGU graveyard decks.” What Khans block actually codifies is a narrower package: a black-centered clan using undead labor, decadent court structures, and the graveyard as a means of **acceleration, attrition, and inevitability**. **Commander Sultai**, by contrast, is a much wider space of BGU tools acquired over many years of Magic history. citeturn7view2turn15view1turn23search0turn26search4

EDHREC makes that widening visible. Its current Sultai commander page shows the color identity led not only by Tarkir legends like **Sidisi** and **Tasigur**, but also by **Muldrotha, the Gravetide**, **Yarok, the Desecrated**, **Zaxara, the Exemplary**, **The Mimeoplasm**, and newer entries like **Teval** and **The Wise Mothman**. That already tells us that, in player practice, “Sultai” means a large family of graveyard, ETB, lands, X-spell, toolbox, and combo shells rather than one lore-locked Tarkiri archetype. citeturn23search0turn26search2

Theme pages deepen the point. EDHREC’s Sultai tags highlight **mill**, **reanimator**, **lands matter**, **proliferate**, **toolbox**, **blink**, **discard**, **ETB**, and **big mana**, while the Sultai combos page is populated by loops built around infinite ETB/LTB, landfall, recursion, or mana. Its Sultai reanimator page features staples like **Animate Dead**, **Dread Return**, **Buried Alive**, **Rise of the Dark Realms**, **Sidisi**, **Hermit Druid**, and **Sultai Ascendancy**. Modern player Sultai, then, is partly graveyard identity and partly “the color combination with the best access to recursive value, interaction, and inevitability.” citeturn26search4turn24search0turn26search1

Official product design now acknowledges that community reading. Wizards’ **Tarkir: Dragonstorm Commander** product includes a Sultai deck called **Sultai Arisen**, and the official decklists page identifies it as black-green-blue and confirms **Zombie Druid** token support. In player-facing coverage, Tolarian Community College summarized that precon as a **graveyard deck built around self-mill and recursion**. That is modern Sultai Commander in one sentence, but it is a Commander-facing condensation, not a complete statement of original Tarkir lore. citeturn42view2turn38search0

## Community Interpretation, Themes, and Cautions

Among players today, “**Sultai**” often functions first as a **Commander color-identity shorthand** and only second as a Tarkir proper noun. EDHREC’s commander rankings and theme pages show that players now attach Sultai to graveyard recursion, mill, reanimator, lands-based value, ETB engines, proliferate, toolbox decks, and combo shells. That is not a mistake; it is the cumulative result of years of BGU card design. But it does mean the player meaning of Sultai is broader than “the Khans clan with delve.” citeturn23search0turn26search2turn26search4turn24search0turn26search1

Reddit discussion reflects that same widening. Players talk about **“Sultai value,” “Sultai goodstuff,”** and the danger of decks like **Muldrotha** or **Tasigur** becoming a “directionless pile” of strong cards if they are not tightly themed. Other threads treat **Sidisi** almost interchangeably with **self-mill reanimator**, and treat **Tasigur** as a politics/control engine that can turn the table into part of the recursion loop. These are not canon statements, but they are useful evidence for how the player community experiences the color identity. citeturn27search0turn27search4turn27search9turn27search13turn40search2turn40search3turn40search9

At higher power, **Tasigur** still carries a specific multiplayer reputation. CompetitiveEDH discussion in the sources reviewed presents him as interactive, politically flexible, and still viable in some metagames, often because repeated recursion from the command zone can turn the graveyard into a dense control/combo resource. That is a real modern player reading of Sultai, but it is very specifically a **Tasigur** reading, not a universal statement about every Sultai deck. citeturn40search0turn40search4turn40search8turn40search16

Player-facing media also now packages Sultai this way. Tolarian Community College’s overview of **Sultai Arisen** describes the deck as self-mill plus recursion and value, which lines up almost perfectly with EDHREC’s broad Sultai graveyard/value usage. That framing is excellent evidence for current player culture; it is not evidence that original Khans canon can be reduced to “generic graveyard deck.” citeturn38search0turn42view2

**What not to overclaim**

The evidence supports several important limits.

- Do **not** say **Sidisi** represents all Sultai philosophy across all timelines. Khans-era **Sidisi, Brood Tyrant** and Dragons-era **Sidisi, Undead Vizier** occupy very different political positions. citeturn11view0turn12view0turn29search2

- Do **not** treat **Silumgar’s brood** as identical to the original **Sultai Brood**. The altered clan inherits geography, necromancy, and opulence, but its hierarchy is dragonlord-first and the original Sultai is explicitly treated as a prior, nearly erased formation. citeturn12view0turn13view0

- Do **not** treat all **black-green-blue cards** as Sultai-lore cards. Much of modern “Sultai” deckbuilding comes from later Commander design rather than Tarkir fiction. citeturn23search0turn26search4

- Do **not** collapse Sultai into “just graveyard value.” Official canon also emphasizes **hierarchy, wealth extraction, palace spectacle, propaganda, poison, hypnosis, and undead labor systems**. citeturn10view0turn11view0

- Do **not** assume poison or infect is the defining modern player meaning of Sultai just because poison is canonical to Tarkir’s Sultai. In the Commander sources reviewed, graveyard, value, lands, and combo themes surface much more prominently. citeturn11view0turn26search4turn24search0

- Do **not** treat **Tasigur’s** politics/control/cEDH reputation as the full meaning of Sultai. It is a powerful branch of player interpretation, not the whole tree. citeturn40search0turn40search9turn40search16

- Do **not** read the **reformed 2025 Sultai** back into the original brood without qualification. That later clan explicitly reacts against both Silumgar and the old Sultai elite order. citeturn42view0

**Best short definition**

Sultai is Tarkir’s **black-green-blue clan of ruthlessness**: in the Khans timeline, a decadent necromantic empire that converts corpses, labor, land, and knowledge into political power under a hierarchy of wealth, fear, and manipulation. In design terms, it is a **base-black** faction whose blue planning and green acceptance of predatory natural systems make the graveyard a usable resource rather than a sacred boundary. In modern Commander culture, “Sultai” has expanded into a broader BGU shorthand for recursive value, self-mill, control, and combo, but that is a player-community extension of the original Tarkiri identity, not its replacement. citeturn10view0turn11view0turn7view2turn33view3turn23search0turn26search4

- Official sources richly support Sultai’s lore, but Wizards does **not** provide a single explicit canonical essay on “what Sultai lacks because it lacks white and red.” That part of the analysis is therefore a **careful synthesis** from official color-pie commentary rather than a one-paragraph canon declaration. citeturn32view0turn32view1turn32view2turn32view3turn32view4
