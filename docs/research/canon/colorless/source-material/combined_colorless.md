## The Metaphysics of the Void: A Ludological and Philosophical Analysis of Colorless Magic

In the structural architecture of the *Magic: The Gathering* color pie, colorlessness is not a sixth philosophy; it is the fundamental absence of one. If the five colors of **WUBRG** represent the spectrum of human emotion, morality, and ideology, colorlessness is the cold reality that exists outside that prism. It operates beyond the psychological conflicts of the color wheel, representing forces that are either entirely artificial, deeply primeval, or fundamentally cosmic.

In narrative and design, colorlessness is split into two distinct philosophical profiles: **Artifacts** (the triumph of mortal ingenuity over natural limits) and **The Eldrazi / The Void** (the terrifying reality of cosmic indifference).

---

*Where the absence of color creates either perfect utility or absolute consumption.*

### The Machine: Artifacts & Construct Colorlessness

* **The Dialectic:** *Ingenuity vs. Identity.* While the five colors argue over *how* to use their souls, the machine operates entirely without one. It represents pure, unaligned utility—tools, constructs, and automation that can be claimed by any philosophy to amplify its own power.
* **Core Drive:** Efficiency, optimization, and the bypass of natural limitations.
* **Belief:** The universe is a collection of parts waiting to be assembled. Ideology is a flaw in the design; function dictates form, and everything can be engineered.
* **Playstyle:** **Fast Mana, Combo Engines, & Voltron.** Bypassing traditional color restrictions to generate immense mana, assembling interlocking pieces of a mechanical engine (the "eggs" or "KCI" styles), or building a single, unstoppable Juggernaut covered in equipment.
* **Emotional Tone:** Amoral, utilitarian, rhythmic, and cold.
* **Vox Mana:** *"The forge does not care who stokes the fire, only that the metal bends."*
* **Key Signals:** Mana rocks, equipment, modular/thopter tokens, cost-reduction effects, and "enters tapped" restrictions.

### The Cosmic: Eldrazi & Void Colorlessness

* **The Dialectic:** *Reality vs. Non-Existence.* This is not a debate between rival worldviews, but the absolute inversion of the color pie itself. The colors represent life and its internal friction; the cosmic colorless represents the vacuum that waits to swallow it all.
* **Core Drive:** Absolute consumption and the erasure of form.
* **Belief:** Reality is a temporary aberration. Colors, morals, and histories are just friction on the surface of a blank canvas that must be wiped clean.
* **Playstyle:** **Ramp, Inversion, & Oppressive Scale.** Utilizing massive, ancient lands (like Urza's Tron or Eldrazi Temples) to cast monolithic, reality-warping threats that punish the opponent simply for existing or trying to interact with the stack.
* **Emotional Tone:** Lovecraftian, alien, inevitable, and utterly indifferent.
* **Vox Mana:** *"It does not hate you. You do not hate the dust you sweep from your floor."*
* **Key Signals:** Wastes (basic lands), Devoid (having no color despite colored mana costs), annihilator/exile mechanics, and cast-trigger distortions.

Phyrexia represents the ultimate, terrifying synthesis of **The Machine (Artificiality)** colliding with the **WUBRG Color Pie**.

Unlike the Eldrazi—who exist completely outside of the colors and seek only to erase them—Phyrexia does not want to destroy the color pie. It wants to **infect, subjugate, and distort it.**

Phyrexia strips the soul, free will, and empathy from the colors, leaving behind a grotesque, hyper-utilitarian carnal house. It bends all five colors toward a single, horrifying overarching thesis: *The flesh is weak; the machine is absolute perfection; All Will Be One.*

Here is how Phyrexia fractures and subverts the color pie across its distinct eras and factions.

---

## The Core Philosophy: "Compleation"

In standard Magic, the colors represent different answers to how an individual or society should grow. Phyrexia offers an unyielding, terrifying answer: **forced unification through biomechanical assimilation (Compleation).**

Mechanically and philosophically, Phyrexia is defined by two foundational components:

* **Glistening Oil:** The viral, toxic mutagen that carries the Phyrexian consciousness. It turns natural biology into a blend of dead flesh, porcelain armor, copper wiring, and black ichor.
* **Phyrexian Mana:** The mechanical manifestation of their philosophy. It allows a player to pay **2 life** instead of colored mana. Philosophically, this represents the classic Phyrexian bargain: *flesh and vitality are expendable resources; the spell will happen regardless of natural constraints.*

---

## Old Phyrexia: The Pure Black Monolith

Under its original creator, Yawgmoth, Phyrexia was anchored entirely in **Mono-Black**. It was the absolute, logical extreme of Black’s core philosophy: *Power through ruthlessness.*

* **The Paradigm:** Yawgmoth viewed organic life as a flawed, diseased draft. Old Phyrexia was a nine-layered mechanical hell designed to systematically harvest, hollow out, and rebuild organic matter into an optimized, cybernetic army.
* **The Distortion:** It took Black's fear of death and desire for self-preservation and magnified it into an immortal, plane-devouring engine. It removed Black's focus on individual free will, transforming it into a hive-mind bound to the Grand Evolution.

---

## New Phyrexia: The Twisted Color Pie

When the glistening oil infected the artificial plane of Mirrodin, it encountered the plane's five mana suns. For the first time, Phyrexia fractured into the five colors, creating the **Five Praetors**. Each Praetor took their color's noblest virtues and weaponized them into a specialized form of tyranny.

### 🏛️ The Machine Orthodoxy (White) — Elesh Norn

* **The Distortion:** White desires *peace through order and community*. Elesh Norn twists this into a blinding, terrifying religious fascism. To her, true unity can only exist if every single being shares the exact same physical and mental template.
* **The Reality:** Individual identity is a sin. If your skin is not peeled away and replaced with sacred, white porcelain-metal plates, you are a heretic.
* **Vox Mana:** *"We are a single body. Flay away the skin of isolation so that we may weave you into the grand tapestry."*

### 🧪 The Progress Engine (Blue) — Jin-Gitaxias

* **The Distortion:** Blue desires *perfection through knowledge*. Jin-Gitaxias strips away Blue’s academic ethics, turning it into cold, relentless, industrial vivisection.
* **The Reality:** The universe is a grand laboratory, and all living things are just raw data to be cut open, studied, and re-engineered on an operating table. There is no room for systemic inefficiency.
* **Vox Mana:** *"Greatness does not wait for evolution. We will carve perfection directly into your bones."*

### 💀 The Seven Steel Thanes (Black) — Sheoldred

* **The Distortion:** Black desires *power through opportunity*. Sheoldred’s faction represents the old-school, predatory nature of Phyrexia—paranoiac, cruel, and fiercely capitalistic.
* **The Reality:** Absolute social Darwinism and literal slavery. Power is the only metric of worth; the weak exist solely to be dominated, harvested for parts, or used as footstools for the strong.
* **Vox Mana:** *"Bow your head and accept the collar, or lose it entirely. Those are the only choices in the dark."*

### 🌋 The Quiet Furnace (Red) — Urabrask

* **The Distortion:** Red desires *freedom through action and emotion*. Because Phyrexia is fundamentally an orderly, unyielding machine, Red became Phyrexia’s **systemic anomaly**.
* **The Reality:** Urabrask and his forge-dwellers value industry, but they fiercely guard their own independence and emotional autonomy. They refused to force compleation on the Mirran survivors, choosing a policy of aggressive isolation: *"Leave them alone, as long as they stay out of the forges."*
* **Vox Mana:** *"Let them run. We have an empire of iron to forge, and we do not need their fragile souls clogging the gears."*

### 🌲 The Vicious Swarm (Green) — Vorinclex

* **The Distortion:** Green desires *harmony through acceptance of the natural order*. Vorinclex strips away Green's reverence for balance and eco-stability, reducing it to pure, animalistic apex predation.
* **The Reality:** Survival of the fittest taken to its absolute, cybernetic extreme. Sickness and vulnerability are crimes against nature. The strong must hunt, consume, and integrate the weak to continuously optimize the biological ecosystem.
* **Vox Mana:** *"If you cannot fight, you are food. If you cannot adapt, you are scrap. The evolutionary ladder has no room for the stationary."*


To understand the Eldrazi, one must understand that they do not exist *within* the Multiverse; the Multiverse exists within *them*.

If the five colors of **WUBRG** are the musical notes that compose the symphony of reality, the Eldrazi are the absolute, crushing silence of the vacuum outside the concert hall. They are not creatures of flesh and blood, nor are they spirits or traditional gods. They are extra-dimensional, titanic entities originating from the **Blind Eternities**—the chaotic, non-space that separates planes of existence.

When an Eldrazi appears on a plane, what mortal eyes perceive (a mountain-sized mass of tentacles, floating geometric obsidian, or chitinous limbs) is merely a projection. It is a single finger dipping into a pond; the actual body remains outside, incomprehensible and vast.

*The cosmic functions of consumption, distortion, and replication.*

### 🦈 Ulamog: The Infinite Gyre (The Hunger)

* **The Paradigm:** Ulamog represents the physical destruction and material consumption of reality. He is the cosmic stomach of the void. When Ulamog walks a plane, his presence strips the landscape of its vital essence, leaving behind a sterile, chalky white dust known as "bismuth-like waste." He does not care about your history, your magic, or your life; you are merely fuel to be processed back into nothingness.
* **The Mechanical Inversion:** In standard MTG, resources are safely hidden in the library or graveyard. Ulamog shatters this safety by weaponizing **Exile**. He doesn't just kill creatures; he erases them from the game entirely.
* **Playstyle:** **Inexorable Inevitability & Destruction.** Casting an Ulamog card instantly dismantles the opponent's board before the spell even resolves. His lineage utilizes the **Ingest** and **Processor** mechanics—eating the opponent's deck and utilizing their exiled cards as literal currency to power their cosmic abilities.
* **Emotional Tone:** Unblinking, ceaseless, mechanical starvation.
* **Vox Mana:** *(The Eldrazi do not speak, but the wind through the ash whispers:)* *"That which was solid is now dust. That which was real is now gone. The hunger is never full."*
* **Key Signals:** On-cast triggers that destroy or exile permanents, attacking clauses that exile the top cards of libraries, and massive indestructible bodies.

### 💎 Kozilek: The Butcher of Truth (The Distortion)

* **The Paradigm:** Kozilek represents the breakdown of the natural laws of physics, time, and sanity. If Ulamog destroys the physical matter of a plane, Kozilek destroys its internal logic. His presence warps local gravity, turns linear time into a closed loop, and distorts the perceptions of living beings until their minds shatter. His physical manifestations are surrounded by floating, impossible crown-like shards of black obsidian that hover in perfect, unnatural defiance of local physics.
* **The Mechanical Inversion:** Kozilek subverts the fundamental math of the game. He manipulates the stack, turns hand-sizes into weapons, and counters spells not through blue magic, but by matching the mathematical CMC (Converted Mana Cost) of the opponent's thoughts.
* **Playstyle:** **Mind-Warping Control & Paradoxical Draw.** Kozilek decks focus on explosive card drawing to refill their hands instantly, paired with the psychological terror of the **Annihilator** mechanic—forcing opponents to sacrifice their own lands and hard-earned permanents just for the privilege of blocking.
* **Emotional Tone:** Vertiginous, surreal, hallucinatory, and deeply unsettling.
* **Vox Mana:** *"Look upon the geometry of the void and realize that your truth is a lie. Up is down, down is out, and your mind is ours."*
* **Key Signals:** Mass card draw upon casting, counter-magic based on mana value, and reality-bending static abilities that dictate how opponents can or cannot block.

### 🐙 Emrakul: The Promised End (The Corruption)

* **The Paradigm:** Emrakul is the ultimate, terrifying apex of the trio. She represents flesh-warping mutation, eco-assimilation, and the absolute domination of the biological mind. She does not leave behind dead ash like Ulamog or weird rocks like Kozilek; she forces all organic matter on a plane to reshape itself in her image. Under her influence, birds, wolves, humans, and trees melt together into grotesque, tentacled horrors with latticed flesh, bound to her singular, sweeping consciousness.
* **The Mechanical Inversion:** Emrakul breaks the ultimate boundary of the game: **Player Autonomy**. She crosses the table, strips you of your free will, and plays your turn for you, forcing you to ruin your own strategy, sacrifice your own creatures, and sabotage your own destiny.
* **Playstyle:** **Mindslavery & Tribal Fusion.** Emrakul acts as the ultimate late-game nuclear option. Her brood manipulates the graveyard through mechanics like **Delirium** or fuses multiple permanent types together into monstrous **Emerge** mutations, allowing massive threats to burst out of the chests of smaller creatures.
* **Emotional Tone:** Maternal yet horrific, suffocating, ecstatic, and inescapable.
* **Vox Mana:** *"Do not fight the tide. Melda with the soil. Melt into the sea. We are the family, we are the flesh, All Will Be Emrakul."*
* **Key Signals:** Protection from instants/colored spells, taking control of target player's next turn, cost-reduction based on card types in graveyards, and the creation of Eldrazi Horror tokens.

## The Ultimate Ludological Truth of the Eldrazi

In the grand design of *Magic: The Gathering*, every faction—even the villainous Phyrexians or the cutthroat Orzhov Syndicate—requires the game's core resources to function. They need colors, they need infrastructure, and they play by the basic rules of the stack and the battlefield.

The Eldrazi do none of this. They introduce true **Colorless Mana ($\diamond$)** as a strict, unyielding requirement—a resource that cannot be satisfied by standard colored mana. They are the only faction that consistently rewards you for *casting* them rather than *resolving* them, ensuring that even if an opponent counters their physical manifestation, the cosmic ripples of their arrival have already permanently damaged the game state. They are the Multiverse's natural reset button: cosmic janitors tasked with eating dead or broken planes, operating on a scale that turns gods into insects.

# The Metaphysics of WUBRG: A Ludological and Philosophical Analysis of the Magic: The Gathering Color Pie

The structural framework of *Magic: The Gathering* is anchored in the five-color wheel—an elegant, closed-loop classification system that maps human psychology, ethics, and conflict onto competitive game systems. Known by the acronym **WUBRG** (White, Blue, Black, Red, Green), this system transcends mere cosmetic flavor or beginner-level archetypes. It operates as a set of unyielding philosophical truths that govern both narrative design and mechanical play. Each color represents a distinct thesis on how the universe should function, utilizing tailored mechanics to impose its specific worldview onto the game state.

---

## 4. WUBRG Manifest: The Five-Color Sovereign

When all five colors align in absolute, symmetrical perfection, they create **The Sovereignty**. This is not a chaotic, diluted soup; it is the ultimate realization of the Multiverse’s total design. In ludological terms, five-color alignment represents the synthesis of all available mechanical tools and philosophical perspectives.

### The Philosophical Paradigms

Five-color philosophy manifests in two diametrically opposed archetypes:

* **The Nexus of Creation (The Avatar of Balance):** The pure, primordial manifestation of reality itself. It represents absolute equilibrium, containing all conflict, emotion, structure, and entropy within a singular, godlike form. This is the world-soul rising to defend its own existence. It wants nothing because it already encompasses everything.
* **The Grand Tyrant (The Engine of Apex Dominion):** The terrifying realization of a single mind using all five colors as an integrated piano of power. Rather than seeking balance, the Tyrant weaponizes the entire spectrum to serve a singular ego:
* **White** provides the rigid institutional hierarchy and subversion of law.
* **Blue** provides the infinite, hyper-rational calculation and foresight.
* **Black** provides the ruthless, uncompromising self-interest.
* **Red** provides the explosive, volatile passion and destructive drive.
* **Green** provides the raw, apex physical size and biological inevitability.



### The Mechanical Truth

From a game-design perspective, five-color structures present the ultimate risk-and-reward paradigm. They introduce extreme resource vulnerability via the complex engineering required for a five-color mana base (the fragility of choosing "greed" over consistency). However, if this systemic tension is resolved, the reward is total systemic dominion: the ability to cast spells that shatter the fundamental boundaries of the game state, cascade through the library, generate infinite value loops, and answer any threat the opponent presents.

No Color




Yep, you can have an entire deck that does not care what color mana you use or, for added
dif culty, you can have a deck that requires colorless mana. Colorless decks tend to be
either artifact-only decks, ones that use a lot of Phyrexian mana, or everyone’s favorite

skipping the dual or tri-lands unless they have an additional added bonus (like shock lands

Why Color Combos Matter (Sometimes)
The good thing about color combos is you can do with this info whatever you want, really.
Technically speaking, all of the combos are viable ways to play Magic.


Some considerations to keep in mind along with the format you play:


     The more mana colors you include, the more possibility that in a given game, you’ll be
     hoping for access to a color that you don’t have yet.

     While dual- and tri-lands do exist, only the original dual lands are consistently as fast
     and versatile as basic lands.

     Tutoring, while a thing, isn’t equally available in all colors and can take up a place in
     your decks that other cards could better utilize.


A lot of the 3-color decks worked wonderfully in their respective sets and meta-games,
but their mileage may vary outside of them. There is no “wrong” way to play Magic, but
sometimes the synergies and quality of cards aren’t there to make them playable at the
competitive level.


The best suggestion I can make is if you want to be competitive, look up what’s working in
the pro-circuit using the Arena Tutor metagame tab.


The Best Combo for You
Which color combination should you play? That completely depends on your personality
and play style.

If you’re someone who enjoys sitting down and playing mostly with your own toys, then
mono-red, mono-green, Boros, Golgari, or any combinations that are mostly red or green
are most likely your style.


If you want to make sure you get to beat face unimpeded, look at mono-white, mono-blue,
Azorius, or Selesnya.


Want to ruin everyone else’s fun? Look at mono-blue, mono-black, Dimir, or Esper.


What I’m getting at is that there is always a way to play the game that makes it the most
fun for you speci cally. Just don’t be surprised if your brand of fun and someone else’s
don’t play well together.


The Best Color Combo
Objectively, the “best” color combo period is hard to de ne, since every set tends to
change things enough to give each combo their turn in the spotlight. In terms of
popularity, 2-color and 3-color tend to be the combos that get the most play.


Ultimately, the best color combination depends on what speci c criteria you look for.

                        CONCEPT VS. EXECUTION
                         Making Magic Oct 19, 2015


                                  Mark Rosewater




                        Welcome to Devoid/Colorless Week, where we're going to talk all about colorlessness and the devoid
                        mechanic. There are a lot of topics I could touch on today, but I've decided to address an issue that's been
                        brought up a number of times on my social media. Why did Battle for Zendikar have to use devoid? It
                        doesn't do anything. Aren't there better ways to do it than wasting a keyword on it?

                        To address this issue, I am going to talk about a much larger issue, and I will use devoid as my case study.
                        The issue is the di erence between design concepts and nal executions. In this column, I tend to focus a
                        lot on how the design team comes up with ideas, what our inspiration is, and how we weave synergies into
                        our designs. What I spend less time talking about is the huge amount of work behind taking an idea and
                        turning it into something that can actually be made. Today's column is going to focus on this problem,
                        something that is a giant design (and development) issue.



                        "IF YOU BUILD IT"
                        Let's say you go out on the street, nd 100 random people, and ask them to draw the coolest house they
                        can think of. You're not asking them to draw a house that already exists, but to use their imaginations and
                        make a house that has never existed. You then take these drawings to an architect and ask their opinion.
                        What do you think would be the number one comment? Most likely, it would be that many of them look
                        cool but couldn't actually be built. Why? Because a building has to have structural integrity; something has
                        to actually hold it up. When you're brainstorming, a building shaped like an hourglass might sound cool, but
                        is it actually possible? Is there a way to build it so it doesn't just fall over?

                        My point here is that it's easy to come up with ideas that can't practically be executed. The same is true for
                        Magic design. Just because I (and my fellow designers) come up with an idea, it's no guarantee that idea will
                        necessarily work within the con nes of the game. Let's examine a few of the problems you can face:

                               The idea doesn't work within the rules.

                        One day, I made the following card: "Creatures you control with power 4 or greater have ying." The set
                        had a mini-theme that rewarded having bigger creatures, so I made a card that granted them evasion.
                        When I showed this to our rules manager (Matt Tabak), he informed me that it couldn't be done. Why?
                        Because of something called "layers." You see, in order for the game to know what qualities a permanent
                        has (because other things can a ect it), there is a speci c order that qualities get applied. If an e ect
                        creates a relationship between two attributes, the attribute being a ected has to happen at a layer prior to
                        the attribute being added. You determine ying before you determine a creature's power, so the above
                        card doesn't work. The game essentially hasn't yet signed o                   on what creatures have power 4 or greater at
                        the time ying is applied.

                        I've had many cards killed over the years because of rules issues. (Some of which I later put into silver-
                        border sets where we're a little laxer with our rules—I'm a bit more of a laid-back un-rules manager.) You
                        often don't see the problem coming because it concerns some little subsection of the rules that you're not
                        aware of. This problem is particularly true when you mess with virgin design space that the rules haven't
                        had to handle before. The rules manager works hard to add new rules, but the current infrastructure
                        sometimes makes doing so very di cult if not impossible.




                        Council's Judgment | Art by Kev Walker

                               The idea isn't worth the stress on the rules the change would require.

                        Magic has a very robust set of rules, but it has limits. For example, in Future Sight, we tried to make a
                        futureshifted card with "last strike." The idea was very simple. Just as rst strike happens before normal
                        combat damage, last strike would happen after normal damage. We had no problem playing with it. It's
                        logically consistent, and we always knew what happened when it came up in gameplay, but the rules
                        manager at the time (Mark Gottlieb) explained that adding last strike to the game would require a complete
                        overhaul of how combat works. The system was built around normal damage and rst strike. Adding in new
                        layers requires pulling out the existing system and extensively reworking it. Gottlieb felt that the work
                        required to make the change and the impact of the change on the rest of the game wasn't worth it for the
                        new mechanic, so it was abandoned.

                               The card can't be templated.

                        In design, we tend to simply write cards in such a way that people understand what they do. To actually
                        print it, though, the card has to be templated (aka written in such a way that it is consistent with how
                        language works on Magic cards—I call it "Magic-ese"). We're reworking a mechanic right now for an
                        upcoming set because the editors informed us that there wasn't any way to template the cards' current
                        functionality. The mechanic had a few quirky things about it that made it fall through some templating
                        cracks. To keep the mechanic, we had to tweak it so it could be templated.

                               The text cannot      t on the card.

                        When we made the cycle of gods' items in Theros, many people questioned why we didn't make them
                        Equipment, and the answer was because they were already legendary enchantment artifacts. We simply
                        didn't have room on the card type line for the word "Equipment." Often, late in development, cards will be
                        altered because the rules text doesn't t. A lot of people like to think of design as an open-ended blue sky
                        capable of doing anything, but there are constraints that we must meet, and the ability to physically t the
                        rules in the text box is one of those constraints.

                               The card contradicts          avor.

                        In Exodus design, Mirri, Cat Warrior originally had protection from black. One small problem: In the Exodus
                        story, she was killed by Crovax, a character who had recently, through a curse, been turned into a Vampire.
                        Crovax was a mono-black character. Cards have the ability to bend a little on how to interpret avor, and
                        mechanics have a little wiggle room, but this was over the line. It contradicted the story, so we ended up
                        removing protection from black and adding a toughness point.

                               The card has a developmental issue.

                        A very common reason that cards and mechanics get killed in design is because the development team
                        expresses concern that there is no way to make them playable without great developmental risk. Other
                        times, the only way to cost it fairly would make the whole mechanic seem super-unappealing. And
                        sometimes, it messes around in an area that is o                limits because of something we've done previously,
                        which development has to work around. Many designs get killed because they can't be properly developed.

                               The card cannot work in digital.

                        In Future Sight, I toyed around with a cycle of cards based o                 of the Double cards from Unglued. The cards
                        each had an e ect when played and then again at the start of the next game. The Magic Online people
                        came to me when they heard about those cards and said that there was no way for e ects in one game to
                        interact with another game, as the system had no way to connect the two. Making this change would entail
                        a major overhaul in the system, and even then, there was no promise they could get it to work. I removed
                        the cycle. (I should note that it was a contentious cycle anyway, so it might have been killed later for
                        di erent reasons.)




                               The card creates excessive problems in digital.

                        Sometimes the issue isn't that it can't work in digital, but rather that the current incarnation will be
                        problematic. A slight tweak can often take something that would be very di cult to program or something
                        that would be unwieldy in execution for the user and make it go away.

                               The card creates problems in organized play.

                        Just as the cards have to work digitally, they also have to work with the tournament rules. From time to
                        time, we make cards that cause problems because they do something that isn't allowed in the oor rules or
                        cause players to do something that they traditionally aren't allowed to do. Now, most of the time, the
                        tournament rules are able to adapt, but every once in a while it's easier to just tweak the card than majorly
                        overhaul some aspect of tournament play.

                               The card has a brand issue.

                        Unglued and Unhinged both played around in very crazy space, and there were several cards that the brand
                        team asked very politely for us to either severely change or remove.

                               The card has a legal issue.

                        I'm not allowed to give an example for this one, but su ce it to say: It's happened.

                        My point here is that there are many pitfalls along the journey of creating a design and making it work
                        practically. We've started doing more work in design to address all these issues so we can kill things earlier
                        if we know they're not going to work out.



                        COLORLESS ME IMPRESSED
                        So, let's get the actual story of devoid. What happened? How did it end up as a keyword?

                        As I explained in my preview article, I needed a way to unify all of the Eldrazi. In Rise of the Eldrazi, we did it
                        by using the tribal card type to allow every Eldrazi card to have the subtype Eldrazi. We've stopped using
                        the tribal card type, though, so that option was o              the table. The Eldrazi were closely associated with
                        colorlessness, so I was interested to see if we could use that as the mechanical component tying them all
                        together. The problem was, we needed enough Eldrazi to establish them as being the dominant threat on
                        Zendikar, but creating that many cards with generic costs had all sorts of issues (see Mirrodin block for a
                        good example of things going horribly wrong because most of the good cards could t into any deck).

                        The solution to the problem ended up being the futureshifted card Ghost re from Future Sight. It was a card
                        requiring red mana that was colorless. Using this technology would allow us to make all the Eldrazi colorless
                        while not causing color-pie issues because cards would be segregated by their mana costs.




                        The very rst cards we made simply had "This card is colorless" written near the top of the rules text. I
                        quickly decided that I didn't want to have to write that line on every card. There were going to be a lot of
                        colorless cards, and it was adding what I thought were unnecessary words to the set.

                        My original take on it was that the cards were going to physically be colorless. How would players know the
                        card was colorless? They'd look at the frame, and it would be obvious. So I changed all the cards in the le
                        and removed the line "This card is colorless." This created a small problem for playtesting, though.
                        Colorlessness was an important quality mechanically; players had to be able to tell whether or not their
                        stickered cards were colorless. My rst idea was to sticker all of them on colorless cards, but my worry was
                        people would just assume they were stickered incorrectly. The solution I eventually settled on was to use
                        the word colorless on their card type line as if it were a supertype. A devoid sorcery, for example, would
                        read colorless sorcery.

                        To make sure I wasn't being completely unrealistic, I went and talked to Matt Tabak, the current rules
                        manager, to see if we could convey colorlessness in some way other than writing it out. Matt was optimistic
                        we could. All seemed good.

                        The rst sign of trouble didn't actually happen until development. Early in development, they have to sign
                        o    on whether or not we're going to need new card frames, because if we do, we have to start work on
                        them early. I said that I assumed we'd have a colorless frame similar to what we did in Rise of the Eldrazi but
                        adapted to the newer card frame. My assumption was the devoid cards would use the same colorless frame
                        the true colorless cards used. (In design, we came up with the term "true colorless" as a means to discuss
                        the colorless cards that had a generic mana cost.)

                        This all changed when more eyes started focusing on the devoid cards. They were weird, and they rubbed a
                        bunch of R&D people the wrong way. Were they necessary? Yes, I said, both because I wanted to be able to
                        mechanically tie all the Eldrazi spells together, and because the "colorlessness matters" theme was a strong
                        one in the set—something that was helping give Eldrazi a strong mechanical identity, especially in Limited.
                        So we talked to the people who were concerned and began to understand their issues. The biggest was that
                        in your hand, they wanted the card to communicate that it required colored mana to cast, but on the
                        battle eld, it had to be clear that the card was a colorless permanent. How could a card be "colored" in
                        your hand and colorless in play?

                        That was the challenge presented to Liz Leo, a member of the art team that focuses on larger graphic-
                        design issues like frames. Liz came up with a clever solution. What if the card title looked like a title bar on
                        a colored card but the rest of the card below it looked more like a colorless card?




                        I'll use Nettle Drone as my example. In your hand, Nettle Drone seems like a red card.




                        But on the battle eld, it appears more colorless.




                        Okay, we had the frame worked out. We were good to go, right? Not exactly.



                        COLORLESS OF THE WIND
                        Here was the problem. The rules say there are three ways to determine color. One, it can be de ned by the
                        colored mana (or lack of colored mana) in their mana cost. That didn't work on devoid cards, as they were
                        not the same color as the colored mana in their mana cost. Two, it can be stated in the rules text. I didn't
                        want this one because I was trying to keep the devoid cards from being cluttered with what I felt was
                        unnecessary text. Three, cards can have what is called a color indicator. I think they rst came about
                        because we had double-faced cards that had a side that didn't have a mana cost, and we didn't want to
                        have to write it out in their rules text, so we used a colored circle right before the word "Creature" on the
                        card type line to indicate color.




                            Transform                            Transform


                        If the rst answer didn't work and we didn't want to use the second, that meant our only answer was to
                        make the third option work. We needed to make a color indicator that conveyed colorlessness. Liz worked
                        on some executions, and then we tested them on Wizards of the Coast employees who were Magic players
                        but had never seen devoid cards. Here's what we learned: It is near-impossible to convey with a colored
                        circle the concept of colorlessness. If it's gray, it reads as gray. If it's brown, it reads as brown. If it's see-
                        through, it reads as whatever color is in the art below it. We tried test after test, and it became clear that
                        people just weren't getting it.

                        Once the third option had been ruled out, it became clear that the second option was our only way of
                        making it work. We were going to have to use words. The rst thing we did was to come full circle, back to
                        where we began, and use the text "This card is colorless."

                        We then did another round of testing and found a new problem. When we showed new players the cards
                        and asked, "Is this card colorless?" they said yes. But if we then asked, "Is this card red?" (for a devoid card
                        with red in its mana cost), they also said yes. Players believed it was possible for cards to be both colorless
                        and a color.

                        The rules text we wanted to use, "This card has no color," doesn't actually work as stand-alone rules text. It
                        uses vernacular that might be easier to understand but doesn't follow the programming language that is
                        Magic templating. Tim Aten, the editor on the set, came up with the solution. If we keyworded it, we could
                        use the line we wanted as reminder text. Reminder text can be looser in structure than actual rules text. In
                        addition, keywording it solved another problem we were running into. How could players talk about devoid
                        cards without an assigned name? Calling them colorless cards was incorrect, because the set was full of
                        true colorless cards, cards that are colorless with generic mana costs. Keywording the ability allowed players
                        to discuss the subset as a thing.

                        And that is how an idea that started o           as one concept ended up being executed very di erently. Why does
                        devoid have to be the way it is? Because every other version of it we tried failed.



                        THE SOLUTION THAT WORKS
                        Today's column isn't just about devoid. It's about how game design (any design really) has to face certain
                        truths. It's wonderful to have a cool idea, but if that idea can't be practically applied, it's of no use. The key
                        is taking your good idea and guring out what you need to do with it so that someone else can actually use
                        it. It's not always pretty, and sometimes you end up far away from where you started, but it's a painful
                        truth of creation. Concepts are great, but a concept without an execution is nothing more than a dream. If
                        you want to work in a eld where you produce tangible results and a nished product, you have to face the
                        reality of making compromises to make your thing work. Devoid is just the latest Magic example of this
                        principle in action.

                        That's all I have for today. I hope the story of devoid was illuminating. As always, I'm eager to hear your
                        feedback. You can write to me through email or any of my social media (Twitter, Tumblr, Google+,
                        Instagram).
